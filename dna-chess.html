A game of molecular sabotage

DNA Chess Run the Central Dogma as a war machine. Give your opponent cancer.

Your back rank is your genome — eight genes, eight lives. Switch them on to transcribe RNA, translate RNA into protein enzymes, and march those enzymes across the cell to edit the enemy's DNA. Mutate more than half of their genome and their cell loses control: cancer, and the game.

02The molecules

Movement is given by chess analogy so it's fast to learn; the action is where the biology lives. Proteins are built by translating RNA — there is no fixed army, you express the one the position needs.

03How a turn runs

"As time goes on, DNA produces RNA out of each square, moves one square, and takes an action." — formalized into three phases.

➀ Transcription (the tick)

Every active gene automatically spawns one RNA on the empty square in front of it — the board grows on its own. A gene that's blocked (something sits in front) or silenced stays quiet. Cap: one un-used RNA per gene, so the cell never floods.

➁ One action

Then you pull one deliberate lever:

  • Move + act with one piece (e.g. slide a Nuclease in and mutate a gene), or
  • Translate RNA into a protein of your choice, or
  • Repair a damaged gene with a Ligase.

➂ Decay (upkeep)

RNA is unstable. Any RNA that sits unused for 3 of your turns degrades and is removed. Use-it-or-lose-it keeps the game moving and stops turtling. (Simple variant: no decay, just cap RNA at 6 per side.)

04Winning: cancer

You lose the moment 5 of your 8 genes are mutated — strictly more than half. Regulation collapses, the cell goes cancerous. So: race to mutate five of your opponent's genes before they mutate five of yours.

Opponent's genome — 5 of 8 mutated tips them into cancer. Silenced genes (switched off) do not count; only permanent mutations do. Only a Ligase, reaching a fresh lesion in time, can pull a gene back.

05Why it works & what's still open

The core tension

  • RNA is dual-use: spend it to build a protein, or use it to silence a gene and degrade enemy RNA. Every transcript is an offense-or-defense decision.
  • Mutations are permanent unless a slow, short-range Ligase reaches them in time — so you can't just turtle.
  • Tempo is king: a Kinase can grant a second action, turning a 3–mutation position into a lethal 5 in one swing.

Open questions for playtest

  • Does the transcription tick still clog the board? (fallback: genes transcribe every other turn)
  • First-player advantage — use a pie rule, or give player 2 a free starting RNA?
  • Should proteins also denature after N turns so the board keeps turning over?
  • Repair window & Nuclease cost are the two big balance dials — tune together.
